We define ourselves by our jobs. They are the cornerstone of our identities.
If they weren’t, then why is asking someone what they do for a living as
trivial as asking where they’re from or what food they like?
Having spent many years in Mexico, I couldn’t even begin to tell you what

getting to know a person as a person.
Other countries like France, Germany, and Japan have similar cultures; it can

After all, what does it matter? Isn’t it secondary to who we are as humans?
Isn’t a job something we all have to do so that we can spend the rest of our
time doing the things we actually like to do?

When I worked at a grocery store deli, people seemed to feel pity for me.
Even though an artist makes way less than just about any job imaginable,
working at a deli in my 30s seemed to get me treated like a deadbeat dad with
a drinking problem. Customers had no issue yelling at me like I was their
unemployed grand son.
Every line of work provokes a different reaction in people, but I typically
never dare to be honest. If I did, I would say something like, “Jobs are
secondary to me doing what I want with life. I just want enough money to
survive and keep doing what interests me. Career be damned.”
But of course, that’s not exactly what people want to hear.
It’s hard to resist this sweeping pathology. Even as somebody who thinks job
titles are BS, I have still laid awake at night, beating myself up over my work
status. Tormented by not having anything to show for myself in terms of
career feats.

Maybe it’s the compulsory nature of it, the idea that it’s something you have

beginning of it all, constantly in trouble and not completing homework in
elementary school, to struggling and eventually dropping out of college.

I remember a friend of mine who was in a bit of a personal crisis dropped
everything to move out of state and attend a doctoral program. It was
grueling. His fellow cohorts would sometimes sleep at school because of
how crazy the hours could be. At some point, he realized it wasn’t for him,
and he threw in the towel.
“You know what I realized?” He asked me.
“More than the actual work of being a doctor, I think I just wanted the
recognition and prestige of being a doctor”.
This is something I could pretend to stand in judgment of, but if I’m honest
with myself, I know that even having a degree carries a similar subjective
value.
Just saying “I graduated college” or “I have a degree” can separate me in the
minds of others, and as messed up as it is, I also wanted that recognition and
validation.

I hate this subtle, invisible competition we all seem to be in in the USA. I
don’t care to play the game, I just want enough money to not die.
To even say that I don’t value a career or wealth makes me feel a subtle sense
of shame, like it’s something I shouldn’t say, but it’s true.
I want enough money to sustain myself so I can continue doing what I love,
which is reading, writing, and art.
One day, I will eventually move out of the country completely. It has been a
slow process, but the older I get, the less and less I can see myself coexisting
in such a country.
