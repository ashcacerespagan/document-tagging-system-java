Before the Kanye features, before the solo careers, before “Grindin’” became
a lunchroom beatbox anthem, there were two brothers from Virginia Beach:
Gene and Terrence Thornton, better known as No Malice and Pusha T. And
No Malice, who at the time went by a simpler, darker pseudonym: Malice.
Together, they formed the Clipse — a duo that built their name on sharp
lyricism, vivid street narratives, and a sound that defined an era of hip-hop.
But beneath the slick wordplay and menacing production was a deeper push-
and-pull: Pusha T delivered verses soaked in cold confidence and decadence,
while Malice served as the moral compass, questioning the very world they
rapped about.
Their early mentorship under Pharrell Williams and the Neptunes didn’t just
shape their sound — it launched them to commercial success. Pharrell’s
minimalist, hard-hitting beats paired with their lyricism culminated in
anthems like “Grindin’,” but it was their guest spot on Justin Timberlake’s
“Like I Love You” that briefly placed them at the center of pop culture. For a
moment, the Clipse weren’t just underground heroes — they were
mainstream fixtures, without ever compromising their complexity.

Two sides of the same cipher: even in their youth, Malice’s measured caution met Pusha’s unrelenting hunger,
shaping a lyrical push-pull that defined an era
Their debut, Lord Willin’, showcased hunger. But Hell Hath No Fury elevated
them to something more profound. Every song felt like a dialectic: Malice
wrestling with faith and consequence, while Pusha luxuriated in vice. It
wasn’t just coke rap — it was existential. The brothers weren’t just rapping at
each other; they were rapping through each other, each verse posing a
question the next tried to answer or evade.
Their dynamic continued to evolve in their fourth album, Til the Casket Drops.
The lyrical interplay remained sharp, but the album’s polished production
and more reflective tone hinted at a growing philosophical divide. Tracks
like “Freedom” showcased Malice’s introspection at its most unfiltered, as he
grappled openly with the weight of consequence and spirituality. Meanwhile,
Pusha remained unapologetic, reveling in sharp precision and defiance,
making the contrast between them feel more pronounced than ever.

Artistically, Kanye and Pusha forged a dynamic rooted in contrast. On tracks
like Runaway, Kanye bares his vulnerability, riddled with regret and self-
awareness, while Pusha glides in with sharp confidence and unapologetic
ego, amplifying Kanye’s emotional unraveling. Where Kanye spirals, Pusha
stands unmoved — his presence acting as both foil and reflection of Kanye’s
inner conflict.
In New God Flow, their partnership becomes almost mythic — Pusha’s
precision and braggadocio paired with Kanye’s expansive ambition to
transform street narratives into gospel-like proclamations. By “Feel the
Love,” the chaos reaches a fever pitch — Pusha opens grounded and
declarative, while Kanye’s wild, wordless ad-libs inject primal energy.

From Daytona to It’s Almost Dry, Kanye and Pusha craft soundtracks where decadence meets discipline —

That relationship eventually transcended the art itself. When Kanye’s feud
with Drake reignited, it was Pusha — not Kanye — who delivered the cutting
blow. By the time The Story of Adidon dropped, it wasn’t just another diss. It
was a surgical takedown, executed with the same cold precision Pusha
honed over years, sharpened by every philosophical battle he’d fought since
the Clipse era.
But to understand Pusha’s penchant for conflict, you have to rewind to where
it all began. His simmering tension with Birdman and Lil Wayne traces back
to an unpaid debt — Birdman’s failure to pay Pharrell for production on What
Happened to That Boy. It’s the kind of industry slight that lingered, festering

Conflict became Pusha’s canvas. Diss tracks were never about petty shots.
They were an extension of the same themes that permeated Clipse records:
pride, betrayal, consequence. He plays the perfect Devil’s Advocate — not in
the traditional sense of defending unpopular opinions, but embodying vice
and confrontation to their fullest extent. He leans into darkness and ego
unapologetically, unbothered by moral consequence. Without Malice
anchoring him, his verses felt freer — and colder. No longer tempered by
spiritual reflection, Pusha reveled in the antagonist role, forcing listeners to
ask themselves what lines are worth crossing.

In 2013, No Malice released Hear Ye Him, a deeply introspective solo album
that marked his pivot toward faith and personal reckoning. Gone was the
braggadocio; in its place, verses filled with reflections on consequence,
morality, and redemption. It was as if Malice’s cautionary tales had reached
their logical conclusion, unspooled without the need for the hustle’s allure.
Where Pusha embraced pride and precision, No Malice dug deeper into the
cost of it all — recognizing that salvation was never a backdrop to the story,
but the ultimate question at its core.
Divergent Paths: Solo Projects
Their respective solo albums reflect this divide. Pusha’s My Name Is My Name
and Daytona are masterclasses in precision and ego — lean, sharp, and
unapologetically cold. Tracks like Numbers on the Boards and “If You Know
You Know” extend the same lyrical confidence that fueled his Clipse verses,
refined and honed for maximum impact.
Meanwhile, Hear Ye Him offers the inverse: subdued production,
introspective lyrics, and a spiritual throughline that cements No Malice’s
journey inward. Songs like “Unforgettable” and “Bury That” reveal a man
grappling with past sins, not glorifying them.

New God Flow
Over the past few years, we’ve gotten glimpses of that tension restored. Their
reunion on Kanye’s Use This Gospel and Pray for You hinted at the old magic.
Pusha, sharpened from his solo reign, and No Malice, more reflective than
ever, still know how to play off each other — one sounding like the tempter,
the other like the confessor.
On Use This Gospel, you hear Pusha in full form, icy and confident, while No
Malice enters with a verse that feels almost like a prayer. The contrast is
undeniable — one brother pushing forward with pride and precision, the
other reminding us that every line carries a cost. Together, they turn the
track into a parable.

Years apart, same tension — the Clipse reunite to ask old questions in new light.
With Let God Sort ’Em Out, Pharrell is back, too, promising to bridge past and
present. But more importantly, it marks the return of their moral friction.
Hip-hop today favors vibe over veracity, melody over message. The Clipse
stepping back in feels almost countercultural. They’re not interested in
nostalgia, but in reigniting the philosophical dialogue they’ve always
embodied: ambition versus consequence, darkness versus light.
They’re not interested in nostalgia, but in reigniting the philosophical dialogue

And for me, it feels like huddling around that Zune all over again — only
now, the stakes feel heavier. Back then, we were just passing earphones,
waiting on the next mixtape, dissecting every line like scripture. We didn’t
realize we were learning something deeper: how to sit with tension, how to
live between sin and salvation.
Now, older, it’s not just about whether they can still rap. It’s about whether
they can still make us wait like that — leaning forward, parsing every bar,
wondering if the next verse might crack something open in us.
These days, the Clipse don’t got it 4 cheap. Luxury coke rap and
philosophical come with a price.But maybe the weight behind it all — the
cost — is exactly the point.
Just like before, we’re all still listening, still anticipating.
