Labor, language, trade — these have been the sacred trinity lifting humanity
from sticks and stones to smartphones and satellites. And if one thing
pushed us forward the fastest, well in the recent years, it was Trade. This
global spiderweb we call “the economy” hasn’t just boosted our bank
accounts; it’s sparked new ideas, built new tech, and raised living standards
across the globe.
If we look back in history, how we handle trade is part of a major ideological
conflict during the Cold War. But even in those antagonistic times,
development continued, though it happened differently in each camp. After
all, struggles often create the conditions for innovation.
But then the Soviet Union bit the dust, and everyone cheered for capitalism.
Francis Fukuyama even called it ‘the end of history,’ a concept developed by
the famous Liberal political philosopher Francis Fukuyama, that liberal
democracy and free-market capitalism had become the endpoint of
humanity… and they all happily lived forever! Peace and progress were
supposed to spread like Wi-Fi with no passwords!
Many believed that globalization, with its interconnected structure and
shared interests, would eventually lead to global peace. However, this has
proven overly optimistic, as conflicts continue to arise, including ongoing
geopolitical tensions between the USA and China. This demonstrates that
history has not ended, and that globalization does not equate to world peace.

For consumers in rich countries, globalization brought an endless supply of
cheap goods, from electronics to clothing, feeding a lifestyle of convenience
and constant upgrades. But for workers in those same wealthy countries?
The story was much bleaker. With factories shutting down and jobs moving
overseas, many workers faced stagnant wages and high unemployment.
Entire industries vanished in places that once depended on manufacturing,
leaving communities hollowed out. Jobs that remained were often low-
paying and precarious, making it harder for people to get ahead. The income
gap widened, with a few at the top reaping the rewards while middle- and
lower-income workers were left fighting over the scraps.
Meanwhile, in developing countries, globalization brought opportunities but
also heavy costs. Workers in places like China, Bangladesh, and Mexico
found jobs in factories making products for the global market, pulling many
families out of poverty. Yet these jobs were often grueling, with low wages,
poor working conditions, and little job security. The environmental costs
were also high, as factories pumped out pollution with minimal regulation.
For many workers, globalization meant exchanging rural poverty for urban
hardship, laboring long hours under harsh conditions, and still struggling to
make ends meet.
So while globalization’s “interconnectedness” spread knowledge, culture,
and investment, it also built a world where profits came first, leaving
workers in both rich and poor countries grappling with insecurity. In
wealthy countries, people felt the sting of lost jobs and a disappearing
middle class; in developing nations, workers paid with their sweat and
health, facing brutal work conditions.

Slowbalization isn’t the end, but a recalibration where countries and
businesses shift gears, taking a harder look at dependencies and risks.
Nations are redrawing the lines, pulling some industries closer to home, and
setting up selective trade with trusted partners. The future of globalization
hangs on this balancing act, caught between economics, geopolitics, and
technology.
Despite all the talk of decoupling, let’s be real: the U.S. and China are still
tied together in ways that can’t just be untangled overnight. Trade between
the two powers hit record highs in 2022, proving that, whatever policies they
throw at each other, their economies are still hooked. Building independent
supply chains might be the goal, but the reality? Both sides still need each
other.

On the global stage, countries allied with the U.S. or China aren’t making
drastic cuts to their ties with either side. Allies still work together on
essential areas like trade, technology, and research. Yes, competition and
tensions are on the rise, but this isn’t a Cold War-style split with rigid,
isolated camps. Still, it’s fair to say the atmosphere is the tensest it’s been

with Russia in China’s corner — shaping global dynamics.
So, what is Slowbalization really? It’s a careful, more cautious version of

connected, adapting their strategies to the new game, not abandoning the
field. Trade may slow, supply chains may shift, but globalization is still
sticking around — just in a tighter, more wary form. In a world of tension
and alliances, full isolation is still a far-off dream.
